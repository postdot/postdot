# ANALYTICS-109 — Map provider engagement analytics and competitor parity boundaries

## Objective and scope

This is a research-only worklog for ANALYTICS-109. It maps provider-owned
engagement analytics and the public parity boundary suggested by Postiz,
Mixpost, and OpenPost. It does not change the operational analytics contract,
provider adapters, schemas, routes, UI, credentials, task board, issue board,
or ADR status.

Repository: Herald
Branch: research/analytics-109-provider-capability-matrix
Base: main
Starting commit: 4106dfbccaf4cf812d6c4629a9bcab57e60cd2ea
Research date: 2026-09-07
Status: complete research artifact; recommendations are non-binding

The current Herald contract is deliberately provider-neutral operational
analytics. The existing surface reports publication lifecycle, queue and
workload state, execution attempts, retries, failures, ambiguity and recovery,
latency, and data-quality or coverage gaps from Herald-owned state and audit
facts. The provider-owned metrics studied here are a different source of
truth: observations returned by a social or content provider about an account,
destination, post, article, message, or other provider content.

“Supported” below means that the provider documents a read path for the stated
subject and metric under stated access conditions. It does not mean that
Herald implements that read path today. None of the six in-scope provider
engagement collectors exists in the current Herald code.

## Evidence method and confidence

The research used the current Herald protocol, issue, proposed analytics ADR,
prior analytics worklogs, provider package contracts, and official competitor
and provider documentation or source repositories. External pages were
reviewed on 2026-09-07. Provider documentation and API schemas can change;
versioned API details are recorded where they were visible.

- High confidence: an official versioned API reference, provider lexicon, or
  provider documentation states the endpoint, field, scope, or limit directly.
- Medium confidence: an official product page, architecture guide, README, or
  source repository describes behavior but omits a normative response contract.
- Unknown: the reviewed official material did not specify the fact. Unknown is
  intentional and must not be converted into a product promise.
- Inference: a conclusion drawn by comparing documented facts, explicitly
  labelled as such. It is not a provider guarantee.

## 1. Executive conclusion

### What “analytics” means for Herald

There are two useful meanings of analytics:

1. Operational analytics answers whether Herald delivered work and what work
   is pending. Its source is Herald state and the audit journal. A provider
   acknowledgement or a provider counter cannot rewrite that history.
2. Provider engagement analytics answers what a provider reports after content
   exists there: followers, subscribers, views, impressions, reach, clicks,
   likes, reactions, comments, shares, reposts, quotes, bookmarks, article
   page views, or provider-specific measures. These are provider-owned
   observations with provider-specific identity, permissions, units,
   aggregation, freshness, retention, and availability.

The proposed direction in ADR-051 and the implementation recorded by
ANALYTICS-108 are therefore sound: keep the operational API unchanged and
place provider observations behind an optional, provider-owned boundary.
Adding provider values to the operational response would make delivery facts
look complete when provider reads are delayed, unauthorized, unavailable, or
semantically incomparable.

### Common competitive expectations

Across the competitor material, the most defensible baseline is not a single
cross-provider score. Users commonly expect:

- account or channel-level counters and publication or content-level
  counters, when the provider exposes both;
- a time range, time series, or at least cumulative values with an explicit
  reporting period;
- the provider content ID and a clear link to a Herald publication when one
  exists;
- the ability to include eligible content not originally published by the
  application, where the provider and permissions permit discovery;
- a visible last-success, freshness, coverage, or next-attempt signal;
- provider-native metrics retained when “reach”, “impressions”, “views”, and
  “engagement” do not mean the same thing;
- missing, not authorized, not supported, stale, rate-limited, deleted, and
  temporarily unavailable states that are not silently rendered as zero;
- collection that is separate from publishing, comments, reactions, inbox,
  direct messages, and other engagement actions.

Postiz documents separate platform and post analytics reads. Mixpost’s
Horizon guide separates provider analytics imports and backfills from
publishing, while its broadcast and inbox queues are separate operational
paths. OpenPost is the clearest reference for an explicit boundary: optional
analytics adapters, optional content discovery, normalized observations and
sync state, and separate comment, engagement, and messaging interfaces.
These are competitor observations, not requirements to copy their data models
or marketing language.

### Adopt

Herald should adopt the following boundary principles for a future design:

- provider engagement is optional and provider-specific;
- account, destination, and publication/content are separate subjects;
- every value carries metric meaning, unit, aggregation or reporting period,
  observed time, fetched time, freshness, provenance, and availability;
- cumulative counters must not be summed or subtracted without provider
  rules;
- a measured zero is distinct from an absent or inaccessible value;
- content identity is provider-owned and must be linked explicitly to a
  Herald publication when the link is known;
- collection is asynchronous, bounded, rate-aware, retryable, and independent
  from the publication effect path;
- stored observations are read separately from the operational analytics
  response;
- provider-native metrics remain available even when a small safe common
  vocabulary is also exposed.

### Avoid

Herald should not:

- add engagement fields to the current provider-neutral operational route;
- promise that every provider supplies reach, impressions, clicks, history, or
  real-time updates;
- treat every counter as an interval metric or add cumulative snapshots
  together;
- equate views with impressions, reach with impressions, or comments with
  inbox state;
- use absent fields, empty responses, or failed reads as zero;
- make outside-Herald discovery a prerequisite for basic publishing
  analytics;
- create one universal provider analytics adapter or one universal metric
  score before the provider-specific differences are accepted;
- backfill historical values that the provider does not expose;
- store raw tokens or raw provider payloads merely to make analytics easier.

### Matrix implications

The providers do not form one equivalent analytics surface:

- Bluesky and X expose useful public post counters, but Bluesky has
  decentralized identity and no documented history while X has significant
  usage cost and private-metric context.
- Mastodon has useful interaction counters but makes the instance part of
  identity and policy, with no central reach or impression contract.
- LinkedIn is comparatively rich for approved member and organization
  analytics, but approval, roles, versioning, retention, and aggregation
  restrictions make it a high-risk first implementation.
- DEV.to / Forem is the strongest history-shaped API in this set for article
  analytics, but exact quotas, response schemas, retention, and account
  authorization need a tighter provider contract before implementation.
- Discord has reaction and approximate audience primitives but does not meet a
  conventional account-plus-publication analytics baseline. Its strongest
  fit is engagement or inbox/event handling.

No row supports a promise of universal reach, impressions, clicks, history,
real-time delivery, or outside-content coverage.

## 4. Minimum competitive baseline

This is the smallest future boundary that would meet the recurring expectations
without pretending that providers are equivalent:

1. Keep the existing operational analytics API and its publication, execution,
   retry, recovery, latency, and coverage semantics unchanged.
2. Add an optional provider-owned read model with account or destination
   observations and publication or provider-content observations. A provider
   can support either subject independently.
3. Require each observation to carry, at minimum, provider, subject type and
   provider id, metric name, value, unit, aggregation meaning, reporting
   period if any, observed time if supplied, fetched time, freshness state,
   availability state, and source or provider-version provenance.
4. Store provider-native metrics instead of forcing false equivalence. A safe
   common vocabulary may include followers, posts, likes, comments, replies,
   reposts, and views only when the provider’s meaning is preserved. Reach,
   impressions, engagement rate, saves, and clicks should remain explicitly
   labelled.
5. Treat a measured zero as valid only when the provider response and metric
   semantics establish zero. Empty, absent, or omitted fields are not zero.
   At minimum, future reads need distinct states for measured, not supported,
   not authorized, not configured, temporarily unavailable, rate limited,
   stale, deleted or private, and unknown. Exact enum names remain an
   implementation decision.
6. Preserve cumulative versus interval semantics. A lifetime counter, a daily
   aggregate, an hourly bucket, and a current snapshot must not be combined
   without provider rules. “Observed at” is not the same as “period ended at”.
7. Link known provider content ids to Herald publications, but do not require
   the link for a provider-owned content observation. Outside-Herald content
   discovery is an opt-in, bounded capability with its own origin and
   confidence metadata.
8. Collect asynchronously with provider-specific pagination, cursoring,
   backoff, Retry-After handling, read budgets, scopes, approval state, and
   cost controls. Reads should use saved observations and expose freshness or
   next-attempt state rather than making a dashboard request wait on a provider.
9. Keep comments, replies as actionable objects, reactions as events, inboxes,
   direct messages, and notification streams in a separate engagement or
   inbox boundary. A reaction count can be an analytics metric; a reaction
   event or moderation action is not the same data.
10. Keep project isolation, credential secrecy, provider retention differences,
    and deletion behavior explicit. Raw tokens and raw payloads are not a
    substitute for a bounded observation contract.

This baseline is deliberately optional. A provider with no standard path for a
metric remains an honest “not supported” result rather than a failed
implementation.

## 5. Recommended future boundary

### Problem and recommendation

The problem is semantic and operational, not merely UI-shaped: provider
metrics arrive from a different authority, have different identities and
aggregation rules, and can be unavailable without implying publication
failure. The recommended future boundary is a separate provider-owned
observation/read model, with separate engagement or inbox interfaces. This
keeps the current operational route provider-neutral and lets providers enter
one at a time.

### Alternatives and tradeoffs

- Add engagement fields to the existing operational route. Rejected because
  it couples delivery truth to provider freshness, permissions, and costs.
  This would be difficult to explain and harder to remove later.
- Define one universal engagement schema and score all providers. Rejected
  because it would erase provider-native meaning and turn unsupported metrics
  into misleading zeros or incomparable values.
- Keep provider analytics entirely in an adapter with no stored observations.
  Rejected because it cannot provide history, freshness, last-success state,
  bounded retries, or a consistent read experience.
- Use a separate provider-owned observation boundary. Recommended. It adds
  future storage and collection work, but is incremental and reversible:
  one provider, one subject type, and one metric family can be enabled without
  changing operational publication semantics.

### Boundary responsibilities

Operational analytics remains responsible for:

- current publications and jobs;
- queue and workload state;
- execution attempts, outcomes, retries, ambiguity, and recovery;
- latency derived from Herald-owned facts;
- data-quality and coverage gaps in those facts;
- the existing authenticated, typed, read-only operational route.

Provider engagement analytics would be responsible for:

- provider account or destination observations;
- provider publication/content observations;
- provider-specific metric names, units, aggregation, and reporting periods;
- provider content ids, account ids, destination ids, and optional Herald links;
- bounded outside-content discovery where the provider allows it;
- provider auth and scopes, approval state, API version, pagination, limits,
  backoff, rate-limit handling, freshness, and availability;
- stored observations and a read path that does not mutate publications, jobs,
  or audit history.

Engagement or inbox would be responsible for:

- comments, replies, reaction events, mentions, notifications, messages,
  threads, moderation actions, and other actionable provider objects;
- event or webhook ingestion and cursor/acknowledgement state;
- provider-specific permissions and privacy handling for those objects;
- write or action workflows, if later authorized.

### Future adapter responsibilities

A future provider analytics adapter should declare and implement its own:

- supported subject types and metric capabilities;
- metric units and aggregation or reporting-period semantics;
- account, destination, content, and publication identity mapping;
- known-content reads and optional bounded discovery;
- required scopes, app review, account roles, plan, and operator policy;
- date ranges, pagination, cursors, and provider-side history limits;
- polling or webhook/event shape, freshness, retry, backoff, and
  Retry-After behavior;
- classification of measured zero, omitted, unsupported, unauthorized,
  deleted/private, stale, rate-limited, and temporary failures;
- provider version and compatibility behavior.

The adapter must not decide that all providers have the same metrics, and it
must not log credentials or turn raw provider payloads into an unbounded
retention obligation. The exact future tables, route names, scheduler, and
common metric vocabulary are not decided by this research.

## 6. First implementation candidate

### Recommendation: Bluesky public account and known-post snapshots

Bluesky is the best first candidate for a small, testable provider-owned
analytics slice:

- Metrics: account followers, follows, and posts; known-post likes, replies,
  reposts, quotes, and bookmarks. This is enough to exercise both account and
  publication subjects while honestly excluding reach, impressions, and
  clicks.
- Access: public profile and post reads do not require a real credential,
  provider analytics subscription, or central app approval. The current
  Herald OAuth contract already knows the atproto scope for publishing, but
  public reads can be tested without it.
- Testability: the current package accepts an injected fetcher. Fixture tests
  can cover AT URIs, DIDs, pagination bounds, blocked and notFound variants,
  and indexer failures without contacting a real PDS or account.
- Shape: DID, handle, AT URI, CID, AppView routing, and possible indexer lag
  make provider identity and freshness visible early. That is a useful
  boundary test rather than an accidental simplification.
- Cost and risk: no reviewed per-resource analytics fee; public API limits
  are visible and operationally manageable for a bounded first slice. The
  tradeoff is no provider-guaranteed historical series and possible AppView
  lag or federation inconsistency.

A reversible first slice would read a configured account profile and a list
of known provider post URIs, persist only normalized observations plus
availability and fetched-time metadata, and expose stored reads. It would not
implement broad discovery, firehose-derived analytics, inbox behavior, or
cross-provider scoring.

### Why not the others first

- LinkedIn has richer analytics but needs Community Management approval,
  analytics scopes, role checks, version discipline, and likely higher
  operational coordination.
- X has strong public counters and private metrics, but pay-per-use reads,
  caps, and context-dependent access make an initial collector expensive to
  exercise.
- Mastodon is valuable but makes every instance a policy and compatibility
  boundary, with no central analytics history.
- DEV.to / Forem is a credible second candidate because it exposes totals,
  historical ranges, and last-day hourly article analytics. Its current V1
  response schema, quotas, retention, and authorization semantics need to be
  pinned down before implementation.
- Discord is better evaluated under engagement or inbox because its audience
  and message reaction primitives do not provide conventional cross-account
  publication analytics.

This candidate is a recommendation for owner review, not an implementation
authorization.

## 7. Open decisions requiring owner input

Only the following decisions remain owner or roadmap decisions; provider facts
and the current operational boundary are not reopened here:

1. Confirm that optional provider-owned engagement observations remain
   separate from the existing operational analytics route.
2. Decide whether outside-Herald content discovery is in the product scope,
   and set bounded discovery, privacy, and retention expectations if it is.
3. Decide whether the future read model exposes a small safe common vocabulary,
   provider-native metrics only, or both with explicit semantic labels.
4. Set operational policy for polling cadence, freshness tiers, provider read
   budgets, X spend caps, LinkedIn approval requirements, and provider
   retention differences.
5. Approve or reject Bluesky as the first implementation candidate; if
   rejected, select whether the business priority is richer approved
   LinkedIn analytics or DEV.to’s article-history surface.
6. Decide whether Discord analytics belongs in the minimum analytics baseline
   or should be handled only through a separate engagement or inbox boundary.

No decision in this list authorizes production code, migrations, routes, UI,
credentials, or provider actions.
