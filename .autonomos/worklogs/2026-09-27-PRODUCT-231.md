- Research completed: 2026-09-27
- Herald revision reviewed: `b208e04e3705d4c8dc2840daa572af04dbe10c66`
- Competitor source snapshots: Postiz `374fb202334a4b6db183f2e44c52c83a9db58a8b` (2026-09-25); Mixpost Lite `df57648b866310446703f5294350552b62735df5` (2026-03-16); Mixpost Pro Team shell `d1a4b49d032350a45f5abf9e22d6a32aa2b7ea5e` and Enterprise shell `48f8329aea4480ba23c7002f8644c68d8836d09c` (2026-07-22); OpenPost `6c9c2dbb89cc06ea2d3b9c9c064d850bcbb2b9bc` (2026-09-26).
- Method: read official product pages and documentation, then inspect upstream source at the snapshots above. This was a static comparison; no competitor instance was installed or exercised.

## Executive finding

These products overlap in scheduling and publishing, but they are not interchangeable product blueprints:

- **Herald** is a lightweight publication orchestrator. Its strongest distinction is a deliberate publication lifecycle shared by automated and manual destinations, with explicit recovery for uncertain delivery outcomes and a low-dependency self-hosted setup.
- **Postiz** sells a broad social-media and automation workspace: provider breadth, scheduling, analytics, team collaboration, integrations, and agent-facing access. Its self-hosted edition is feature-rich, but requires the operator to own more infrastructure and provider-app setup.
- **Mixpost** is a tiered social-management product for individuals, teams/agencies, and SaaS operators. Its free Lite source is materially narrower than the paid Pro/Enterprise offer; the commercial packages cannot be audited from the public Lite repository alone.
- **OpenPost** positions itself as a creator workspace as much as a scheduler: content adaptation, image/video creation, media, publishing, inbox, analytics, and API/CLI/MCP/mobile surfaces. Its deployment story is unusually aligned with small self-hosted setups, while network-specific feature coverage still varies.

The useful outcome is not a feature-count race. The comparison instead identifies product boundaries and a small number of real decisions for Herald. In particular, publication performance analytics (audience views, engagement, and related post outcomes) are different from Herald's current operational publication statistics and Prometheus metrics.

## Important scope and evidence caveats

1. **Mixpost is not one codebase with one feature set.** Its public repository is Mixpost Lite under MIT and explicitly says Lite is distinct from the commercial product. Paid package shells depend on vendor-hosted Composer packages and require a license. Current product pricing lists three publishing destinations for Lite and a wider list plus analytics, engagement, queueing, approval, and AI capabilities for Pro/Enterprise. The Enterprise v7 upgrade guide further says the inbox needs provider-specific scopes and webhooks, and names the networks it supports. Those distinctions are reflected in the matrix rather than attributing the whole commercial offer to Lite source.
2. **Provider counts are not equivalent to working feature breadth.** A network may support publishing but not analytics, comments, inbox, or every media format. OpenPost makes this explicit, and Mixpost's current Engagement documentation lists provider omissions. Herald should count a channel as useful only with the capabilities it actually supports, not by connector count alone.
3. **Marketing scope is not the same as verified behavior.** The source review confirms architecture and named modules but does not install products, connect real social accounts, or validate current provider app approvals. Every provider's actual availability is still subject to the platform's API, app review, scopes, quotas, and changing policies.
4. **“Not found” is deliberately narrow.** It means no first-party feature was found in the reviewed public code/docs at the snapshot, not that the feature can never be added, exists nowhere in a hosted edition, or is impossible through a third-party integration.
5. **Operational analytics are a different product question.** Herald's `/api/projects/:projectId/analytics` summarizes publication lifecycle/execution. Its separate Bluesky collection path stores provider observations, but both are API-only and the latter is limited to Bluesky today. Neither is a finished cross-network page for views, comments, and engagement. The existing distinction is captured in [`docs/capability-evidence.md`](../../docs/capability-evidence.md) and implementation in `apps/api/src/modules/projects/{analytics-routes.ts,provider-analytics-routes.ts,bluesky-analytics-service.ts`.

## What the comparison says about Herald

### Strengths worth protecting

- **The domain model is more explicit than a simple post scheduler.** Herald distinguishes a source post from destination publications, automated from manual delivery, and known failure from ambiguous outcome. That is central to the product promise and should not be diluted to copy a broader marketing checklist.
- **Self-hosting is a product advantage, not just a deployment mode.** Herald can start with SQLite and local files without requiring Redis or a separate database service, while retaining an S3-compatible option.
- **The publication lifecycle is observable.** Durable status, audit history, manual completion, retries, and ambiguity reconciliation support the orchestrator role.
- **The existing REST API and CLI provide a real automation path.** The CLI is intentionally partial and MCP remains deferred; the product need not start more clients just because competitors advertise them.

### Material gaps relative to the products

- **Provider breadth and supported formats:** Herald has six automated provider families, but several competitors expose broader catalogs. This is a real distribution gap only when a target user needs an unsupported destination or format; raw counts are a poor priority rule.
- **Publication-performance analytics:** all three competitors present audience/post analytics more directly. This is particularly relevant because the intended Herald analytics concept is publication results, not internal service telemetry. Herald currently has a narrow API data foundation, not the user-facing outcome.
- **Engagement management:** OpenPost and paid Mixpost offer a provider-dependent inbox; Herald has no equivalent. This would expand Herald from publishing into community management and deserves an explicit scope decision, not an assumed checkbox.
- **Editorial approval:** Herald's access roles are not a publish-review workflow. Mixpost Pro advertises such a flow. This matters if teams need an author/reviewer/publisher path; it is not implied by workspace membership alone.
- **Automation breadth:** RSS ingestion, automatic reposts, and next-slot scheduling are useful for recurring content operations in Postiz/OpenPost. They may not fit Herald's intended source-of-truth role unless recurring content is a real user need.
- **Creator tooling:** OpenPost's image/video studio and Postiz's AI-generation features are substantial adjacent products. They are not foundational requirements for Herald's orchestrator identity; adopting them would add media-processing and provider costs as well as product scope.

## Recommendation

Keep Herald's product center as **a dependable, self-hostable publication orchestrator**, not an attempt to reproduce every social-media-management suite. From this comparison, the strongest next candidate to define is a **publication performance analytics workflow** because it aligns with the owner's stated meaning of analytics and is a clear, user-visible difference across the products. Scope discovery should first establish:

1. which post/account measures users need (for example impressions, reach, likes, comments, clicks, and audience change),
2. which of Herald's supported providers expose each measure and with which app scopes/retention limits,
3. whether a useful first screen can be built from a small truthful subset, with explicit freshness and unavailable states,
4. how persisted provider snapshots affect data volume and collection cadence.

Do not begin with an “analytics for every provider” promise. A narrow provider-by-provider capability table and one end-to-end workflow would expose the real constraints before broadening collection. Engagement inbox and editorial approval should remain separate candidate studies: each changes the product workflow and access model. Do not fold them into analytics or start them solely for competitor parity.
