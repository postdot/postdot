# PERF-617 — Sustained overload and recovery qualification

## Scope and method

Owner requested the next qualification after DB-616. PR #23 remained open at the
start, so measurements use its validated head `a396826` and the harness extension
on the same branch. No runtime behavior is changed by this qualification.

The real API scheduler/worker, manual publication creation, encrypted synthetic
credentials and durable claims remain in use. No real provider or external
network is involved. PostgreSQL runs in an owned disposable local container with
normal durability and automatic statistics enabled. Four projects share the
existing two slots, with one active effect per project.

Each workload offers arrivals for 120 seconds, then observes durable drain for
up to 360 seconds. The first offers 24/s with fixed 100 ms effects. The second
offers 10/s with deterministic 100–500 ms effect delays, and injects serialization
conflicts during seconds 30–35 into worker discovery/claim reads. Claim failures
pass through the real transaction retry adapter; planning reads fail directly.
The original five-minute lease remains in use, so abandoned claims can take that
long to reach their safe recovery outcome.

The harness records one-second scheduler state, created/provider-return counts,
error counts and process RSS, plus individual provider starts. A set of job IDs
checks duplicate effects, independently of the concurrent-project admission check.
It no longer stops observing on the first reported error: terminal scheduler
failure, unfinished durable work, ambiguity, duplicate effects or admission
violations still make a run unsuccessful. A recovered error alone does not.
Drain polls durable pending/running counts once per second, rather than equating
provider returns with committed completion. This adds a read-only observation
query, not a new scheduler wakeup.

## Results

Constant-delay case: all **2,880 jobs succeeded**, with four reported natural
serialization conflicts, zero duplicate provider calls and zero admission
violations. Maximum physical concurrency remained two projects. Arrivals lasted
approximately 120 seconds; 1,438 provider returns completed in that window. The
full durable drain took **338.1 seconds** from the start. Interrupted pre-effect
claims waited for their original five-minute leases, then executed safely once.
Wait p95 was 85.4 seconds; this percentile does not describe the small lease-wait
tail, which remains visible in the timeline. The final scheduler state was
`started`, with no pending/running job remaining. Client RSS peaked at about
94.5 MiB; this is not PostgreSQL server memory or a long-term leak assessment.

Variable-delay case: all **1,200 jobs succeeded**, with zero duplicate provider
calls and zero admission violations. The five-second window caused **18 injected
statement failures**, reported as three scheduler recovery episodes. The scheduler
resumed after the window and ended `started`. At the end of arrivals, 698 provider
calls had returned; the full durable drain completed at **200.0 seconds**. Wait p95
was 76.9 seconds, maximum 79.9 seconds. Client RSS peaked at about 99.0 MiB.

Each project executed exactly its offered share: 720 calls per project in the
constant case, 300 in the variable case. There were no missing or extra provider
calls, no pending/running jobs at the end, and no ambiguous outcomes in either
run. This complements DB-616's deterministic after-effect failure test; it does
not replace that proof with a claim that ambiguity can never occur under load.

The first case's maximum wait was 324.7 seconds, despite p95 being 85.4 seconds.
The lease tail therefore matters for user expectations: recovery restores progress
without making every interrupted pre-effect claim immediately executable. Neither
short median waits nor eventual drain imply a tight publication deadline under
saturation. Queue growth is expected when offered work exceeds two-slot service
capacity; the qualification establishes recovery and effect safety for these runs,
not a higher throughput limit.

## Validation and cleanup

Both workload commands exited successfully. A short PostgreSQL smoke run passed
before the sustained cases. The final SQLite smoke completed all 40 jobs with no duplicates and a healthy scheduler. `bun run check:quick` passes. The owned PostgreSQL container was stopped and removed.
The qualification changes only the opt-in benchmark and evidence, not the runtime
or its recovery policy. The next useful optimization investigation is the long
pre-effect lease-wait tail; changing claim retirement policy requires separate
fencing analysis, rather than shortening leases on the basis of one local run.

## Limits

These are local synthetic workloads and finite observation windows, not a
production sizing guarantee or proof of long-term memory stability. Provider
rejections, media uploads, process crashes, connection outages and shared-server
load are not represented. Injected conflicts exercise recovery but do not model
a specific PostgreSQL lock graph. Claims in durable `running` state may be waiting
for lease recovery even when their provider call has settled; they must not be
counted as simultaneous physical effects.
